Are Our Cities Growing at the Cost of Agricultural Land?

How urban expansion, infrastructure and changing land use can reshape the land around our towns and cities.

Aharavanam | Knowledge and practical perspectives | November 2026

Growing cities need homes, roads, workplaces, schools, hospitals, warehouses and public infrastructure. These are real needs. But cities also depend on nearby rural landscapes for food, water, ecological services and livelihoods. When urban development spreads into surrounding areas, the choices made about land can affect both urban quality of life and the future of local agriculture.

The edges of cities are changing places

Peri-urban areas — the transition zones between cities and the countryside — often experience rapid change. Agricultural plots may be divided, land prices may rise, new roads may alter access, and farms may face pressure from construction or changing labour patterns. Some land is converted to non-agricultural uses; other parcels remain cultivated or shift to different uses. The pattern varies by city and region.

What national land statistics can tell us

The Ministry of Agriculture’s Land Use Statistics for 2023–24 records about 28.55 million hectares under the category ‘area put to non-agricultural uses’. This is a broad national land-use category. It includes more than urban housing, and a single year’s total does not by itself tell us how much farmland was converted recently or how much conversion was caused by a particular city. Care is needed when interpreting the number.

Why the relationship matters

When productive land is fragmented or converted, local farming may become harder. Longer distances between farms and markets can add transport costs. At the same time, well-planned cities can protect open spaces, support urban and peri-urban agriculture, improve wastewater treatment and reuse, and design green corridors that make settlements healthier.

A better conversation about land

The question is not whether cities should stop growing. It is whether growth can be planned with a clearer understanding of soil quality, water systems, floodplains, biodiversity, food-growing areas and the people who depend on them. Local planning maps, land records and environmental assessments matter. Decisions should be based on the specific site, not assumptions about every parcel near a city.

Where food forests fit

On suitable land, food forests and agroforestry can be considered as one productive form of green space. They may be relevant to institutional campuses, community spaces and landowners who want to combine trees and edible plants. They are not a universal solution for land conversion and cannot replace the need to protect high-quality farmland. The right use depends on ownership, local plans, soil, water, access and long-term management.

Remember: A city’s future and its surrounding countryside are connected. Good planning should recognise that connection rather than treat land as an empty space waiting for its next use.
