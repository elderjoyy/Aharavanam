How Changing Weather Patterns Affect What We Eat

A practical look at how heat, rainfall timing, droughts and intense rain can affect farms, food availability and prices.

Aharavanam | Knowledge and practical perspectives | November 2026

Before food reaches a market, it has to grow somewhere. Every crop has a range of conditions in which it can germinate, flower, set fruit and mature. Temperature, rainfall, humidity, sunlight and the timing of each season influence those stages. That is why a change in the pattern of weather can matter even when the total rainfall for a year appears normal.

Heat can affect crops and the people who grow them

High temperatures can increase water demand and place stress on crops during sensitive stages such as flowering and grain filling. Heat also affects livestock and the health and productivity of people working outdoors. The effect is not identical everywhere: crop variety, soil, irrigation, planting dates and local conditions all matter.

Rainfall timing matters as much as the total

A season may receive substantial rain, but if it arrives in a few intense spells, runs off quickly or is followed by a long dry period, farmers may still face water stress. Heavy rainfall can also cause waterlogging, erosion, crop damage and transport disruption. Delayed monsoon onset or an unexpected dry spell can complicate sowing and farm decisions.

What the evidence says about India

The Ministry of Earth Sciences has reported that average surface air temperature over India rose by about 0.7°C during 1901–2018. In March 2026, the Government reported an ICAR assessment of 651 predominantly agricultural districts: 310 were classified as vulnerable to climate change, including 109 very highly vulnerable and 201 highly vulnerable. These are assessments of risk, not predictions that every farm in those districts will fail.

How farmers and communities can respond

Responses need to be local. They may include crop varieties suited to heat or drought, adjusting sowing dates, soil cover and organic matter, rainwater harvesting, efficient irrigation, mixed cropping where appropriate, weather information and access to agricultural advice. Farmers should use recommendations suited to their own district and farm rather than copy a technique simply because it worked elsewhere.

What can a food forest contribute?

A thoughtfully designed food forest can bring together fruit trees, shrubs, herbs and other useful plants suited to local conditions. A mix of species may spread harvests across seasons and create shade and habitat. But trees need water while establishing, and a food forest is not a substitute for climate-resilient farming, public infrastructure or farmer support. It is one complementary way to care for suitable land.

Remember: Weather risk is not a reason for fear. It is a reason to understand local conditions, plan carefully and support the people who grow our food.
