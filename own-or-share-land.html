Can Owning or Sharing a Piece of Land Make a Difference?

Explore the difference between owning land, stewarding a site and joining a shared food-growing space — and the questions to ask first.

Aharavanam | Knowledge and practical perspectives | November 2026

A piece of land may offer space to grow fruit trees, vegetables and other useful plants. But land ownership alone does not make a food-growing project viable. Soil, water, access, location, permitted use, security, maintenance and the time available to care for the site are all important.

Owning land and using land are different decisions

A person may own a plot but not have the time, knowledge or water to manage it. Another person may have the skills and interest but no land. A partnership can sometimes connect these needs, but the responsibilities must be clear. Before committing, check title and encumbrances through qualified local professionals, confirm land-use permissions and understand access rights and applicable local rules.

A shared food forest can widen participation

Shared spaces may be created by resident groups, institutions, landowners or community organisations. People can contribute time, knowledge, tools or agreed costs. A shared model can help people participate even if they do not own land, but it needs transparent governance: who controls the site, who makes planting decisions, who pays for inputs, who maintains it and how produce is allocated.

Questions to ask before joining or creating one

Is the land legally available for the intended use? Who has authority to grant access? Is water dependable and responsibly sourced? Who is responsible for maintenance, fencing and safety? What happens if a participant leaves or the land is sold? How are harvests recorded and shared? Is there a written agreement? These questions protect both the land and the people involved.

Think long term, not just about planting day

Trees and perennial plants take time to establish. A project needs a realistic budget for plants, soil preparation, water, tools, labour, replacement planting and ongoing care. A plan should explain what happens during dry periods, holidays, changes in ownership and periods when volunteers are unavailable.

Aharavanam’s approach

Aharavanam sees several possible ways to participate: grow at home, join a shared community space, or work with a landowner or institution to develop suitable land. Each model should be based on clear expectations and local assessment. No particular land purchase, harvest, return or food supply outcome should be assumed.

Remember: The most valuable piece of land is not necessarily the biggest or most expensive. It is a suitable place that people can access, care for and manage responsibly over time.
