Is the Future of Food Becoming More Uncertain?

India grows enormous quantities of food. Yet the journey from a healthy harvest to an affordable meal depends on more than production alone. Weather, water, land, livelihoods and the way food moves through markets all matter.

Aharavanam | An introduction to food resilience | November 2026

Think about the food on a family’s table: rice or millet, dal, vegetables, fruit, milk and other everyday essentials. We often experience food security as something simple — visit a market, choose what we need and bring it home. Behind that everyday experience is a complex system involving farmers, soil, rainfall, groundwater, seeds, labour, storage, transport, markets and public policy.

That system has achieved a great deal. India is a major agricultural producer, and its foodgrain output has grown substantially. But a strong production record does not mean that every part of the food system is free from risk. The important question is not whether India will suddenly run out of food. It is how well our food systems can withstand changing conditions and make nutritious food accessible and affordable to people.

1. India produces a great deal of food — and that matters

India’s achievements in agriculture should be recognised. Government figures report foodgrain production of about 357.73 million tonnes in the 2024–25 agricultural year, a record at the time of the announcement. The country is also a major producer of fruits, vegetables, milk, pulses and other agricultural products.

This is an important foundation. It reflects the work of farmers, agricultural scientists, institutions, irrigation systems, markets and public investment. It would be misleading to suggest that India is on the verge of a general food shortage simply because climate and environmental pressures exist.

However, national production totals do not tell the whole story. A crop can be plentiful nationally while a particular region experiences losses. A market can have food available while prices put some items beyond a household’s budget. Food security also involves access, nutrition, stability and affordability.

2. Weather is becoming a bigger planning challenge

Agriculture depends on timing as much as it depends on total rainfall. Farmers need suitable conditions for preparing fields, sowing, flowering, pollination and harvesting. A season can receive a reasonable amount of rain overall and still cause difficulties if the rain arrives late, falls in intense bursts or is interrupted by long dry spells.

High temperatures can also affect crops, livestock, farm workers and the amount of water needed. Heavy rainfall may damage standing crops, erode soil or disrupt roads and storage. Dry periods can increase irrigation needs and put pressure on already-stressed water sources.

An ICAR assessment reported by the Government of India in March 2026 covered 651 predominantly agricultural districts. It identified 310 as vulnerable: 109 very highly vulnerable and 201 highly vulnerable. These categories are a warning about exposure and vulnerability, not a prediction that every farm in those districts will fail. The assessment also notes that district-level contingency plans have been prepared to guide local responses.

For farmers, the practical response is better local planning: suitable crop varieties, diversified cropping where appropriate, weather information, soil care, water management, insurance and access to advice.

3. Water is central to the future of food

Every food-growing system has a water requirement. In many parts of India, farmers depend on a combination of rainfall, tanks, canals, rivers and groundwater. These sources are not equally reliable everywhere, and they are also needed by households, industries and ecosystems.

In semi-arid regions, including parts of Telangana and neighbouring states, the choice of what to grow should be connected to local rainfall, soil and dependable water availability. A planting plan that looks attractive on paper may not be sustainable if it assumes unlimited irrigation.

Water-wise agriculture is not one single technique. Depending on the site, it can include harvesting rainwater, improving soil organic matter, mulching, drip irrigation, reducing avoidable losses and selecting crops or trees suited to local conditions. A food forest also needs a realistic water budget, especially while young trees are becoming established.

4. Land use and agricultural livelihoods are changing

As towns and cities expand, land around them can come under pressure from housing, roads, industry and other development. Agricultural land may be converted or fragmented. At the same time, farming decisions are influenced by farm size, access to markets, input costs, labour availability, income opportunities and the aspirations of younger generations.

It is too simple to say that people are no longer farming. Agriculture remains a major source of work and livelihood in India. But rural livelihoods are changing, and many farming families face difficult choices about whether and how the next generation will continue farming.

Preserving agricultural knowledge, supporting viable farm livelihoods and making better use of suitable land are all part of a stronger food future.

5. A harvest is only one part of food security

After crops are harvested, food must often be sorted, stored, processed, transported and sold. Poor storage, transport disruption, seasonal variation or a local production shock can influence what is available and what it costs. Perishable produce such as fruits and vegetables can be especially sensitive to delays and losses.

Household food security therefore depends on several connected factors: reliable production, functioning markets, transport and storage, purchasing power, nutrition and public support. A food forest cannot solve all these challenges, and it should not be presented as a replacement for farmers, markets or public food systems.

6. Where can food forests contribute?

A food forest is a deliberately designed growing space that brings together a range of useful plants — often including fruit trees, smaller trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers and ground-level plants. The mix is chosen for the local climate, soil, water and intended use.

When thoughtfully planned and maintained, a food forest can contribute in several ways:

  • More variety: Different edible plants can provide a range of seasonal produce rather than relying on a single crop.
  • Local learning: Families, students and communities can understand how food grows and what caring for land involves.
  • Living green spaces: Trees and other vegetation can provide shade and habitat, while good ground cover and soil care can help protect the soil.
  • Community participation: People who do not own land may still be able to take part in a shared growing space.
  • Long-term stewardship: Landowners and institutions can explore ways to care for suitable land productively over time.

These benefits depend on good design and ongoing care. A food forest takes time to mature. It needs appropriate species, water, maintenance, protection and a realistic plan for harvesting. It does not guarantee a particular yield, income or level of household food independence.

7. What can an individual or family do?

You do not need to buy land or become a farmer to take an interest in food resilience. Start with practical steps that fit your circumstances:

  • Learn where your regular food comes from and when different foods are in season.
  • Grow a few suitable herbs, vegetables or fruit plants at home if you have the space and water.
  • Learn about soil, composting, rainwater and water-wise gardening before expanding.
  • Join a community garden or shared growing initiative where one is available.
  • If you own or manage land, assess its soil, water, access and permitted use before deciding what to plant.
  • Support farmers and local growing initiatives, and avoid wasting food.
A thought worth remembering: The aim is not to replace the food system with individual gardens. It is to add more knowledge, diversity, local participation and responsible land stewardship alongside the farms and supply chains on which we all depend.

Aharavanam’s perspective: grow food, grow understanding

Aharavanam believes that food forests can become one practical part of a more connected relationship between people, food and land. Some people may grow at home. Others may join a shared community space. Institutions and landowners may explore developing suitable land into food forests with local expertise and a clear maintenance plan.

The first step is education. Understand the local conditions, ask good questions, plan carefully and start at a scale that can be looked after. Resilience is built through many sensible actions over time — not through fear or promises of quick results.
