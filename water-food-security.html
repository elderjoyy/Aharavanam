Water: The Hidden Foundation of Food Security

Why rainfall, groundwater, soil moisture and water planning are central to the food we grow and eat.

Aharavanam | Knowledge and practical perspectives | November 2026

Water is needed to grow crops, raise livestock, process food and keep supply chains working. Some of that water comes directly from rainfall; some comes from rivers, reservoirs, canals and groundwater. The mix varies widely across India. A farming plan that is suitable in a high-rainfall district may be unsuitable in a dry region.

Groundwater is important — but not unlimited

Groundwater supports irrigation, drinking water and many other needs. The Central Ground Water Board’s 2025 national assessment estimated annual extractable groundwater resources at 407.75 billion cubic metres and annual extraction at 247.22 billion cubic metres. The national stage of groundwater extraction was assessed at 60.63%. A national average can hide serious local stress: assessment units are classified separately, and conditions differ between blocks, districts and states.

Rain-fed farming remains significant

The Government of India reported in May 2026 that rain-fed agriculture accounts for nearly 60% of India’s net sown area and contributes approximately 40% of total food production. This underlines why rainfall patterns, soil moisture conservation and local water planning matter. Irrigation is important, but it is not available or affordable everywhere.

Water security starts with the site

Before planting, understand the source and reliability of water, seasonal availability, legal permissions, competing uses, soil depth, drainage and the needs of existing vegetation. A borewell that works in one season may not provide a dependable long-term supply. A project should never be designed on the assumption that water will always be available.

Practical ways to use water more wisely

Depending on local conditions, measures can include capturing rainwater, improving soil organic matter, keeping soil covered with mulch or living ground cover, using drip irrigation where appropriate, fixing leaks, choosing locally suitable species and matching planting density to the water budget. Water harvesting structures need sound design, safe overflow and maintenance; they should not obstruct natural drainage or affect neighbours.

Food forests need a water plan too

Trees can offer shade and other benefits, but young trees need care and often need watering while establishing. A food forest should be designed around rainfall, soil and realistic water availability. Drought-tolerant species are not ‘zero-water’ plants. Species selection, spacing, establishment care and long-term maintenance all matter.

Remember: The best planting plan is not the one with the most trees on paper. It is the one that can thrive within the water the site can responsibly provide.
