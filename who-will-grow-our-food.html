Who Will Grow Our Food in the Future?

A closer look at farmers, rural livelihoods, changing aspirations and how the next generation can stay connected to food production.

Aharavanam | Knowledge and practical perspectives | November 2026

Behind every crop are people making decisions and doing skilled work: preparing soil, selecting seed, managing water, watching for pests, harvesting, sorting and getting produce to market. Farming is knowledge-intensive and shaped by weather, costs, land, markets and family circumstances.

Agriculture remains a major source of work in India

The Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation’s PLFS 2023–24 reported that 46.1% of workers were engaged in agriculture (July 2023–June 2024). The revamped PLFS for calendar year 2025 reported 43.0% (January–December 2025). The survey design and reference period changed from January 2025, so the two estimates should not be read as a perfectly comparable year-on-year decline. The central message remains: agriculture continues to account for a very large share of India's employment.

Why young people weigh their options

Young people make choices based on income, stability, education, access to land, working conditions, social expectations and opportunities close to home. Farming can involve uncertain weather, fluctuating prices, seasonal work and significant responsibility. At the same time, agriculture is changing through technology, mechanisation, new crops, processing, direct marketing and services. The challenge is to make farm livelihoods more viable and rewarding.

Farmers need more than appreciation

A strong food future depends on practical support: reliable local advice, suitable infrastructure, access to markets, storage, credit on fair terms, insurance, water management, research and policies that recognise farmers’ risks. Better value for produce and opportunities for value addition can matter as much as new technology.

There are many ways to participate in food growing

Not everyone needs to become a full-time farmer. People can grow some food at home, join a community garden, learn from local growers, support farmer organisations, volunteer in suitable projects or build careers in horticulture, soil care, nursery management, food processing and agricultural services.

Food forests can reconnect people with growing

A well-managed food forest can be a place to learn about plants, seasons, soil, water and shared responsibility. It can create opportunities for landowners, local practitioners, students and volunteers to work together where arrangements are safe and clear. It should complement — not romanticise or replace — the professional work of farmers.

Remember: The future of food is also about the future of the people who grow it. Respect, viable livelihoods and meaningful opportunities are essential.
